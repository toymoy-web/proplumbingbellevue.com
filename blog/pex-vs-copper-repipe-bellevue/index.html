PEX vs. Copper Repiping: What Bellevue's Older Homes Actually Need

A real, practical comparison for homeowners weighing a repipe decision, not a sales pitch for either material.

By the Bellevue Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

If your Bellevue home is due for a repipe, replacing aging galvanized supply lines with something modern, PEX and copper are the two realistic options, and both are genuinely solid materials. The right choice usually comes down to your home's specific layout and your own priorities, not one material being objectively superior to the other.

PEX: Flexible And Typically Faster To Install

PEX is a flexible plastic tubing that can run in longer continuous lengths with fewer joints than rigid pipe, which generally makes installation faster and means fewer potential failure points at connections. It's become the more common choice for repipes in recent years, largely because of that installation efficiency, which also tends to translate to a lower labor cost for the same job.

Copper: The Longer Track Record

Copper has been used in residential plumbing for many decades longer than PEX, and some homeowners prefer it specifically for that longer track record, along with its rigidity and resistance to certain kinds of physical damage that flexible tubing is more vulnerable to. It costs more in materials and typically takes longer to install given the need for soldered joints at every connection and direction change.

What This Means For A Bellevue Repipe Specifically

Older Bellevue homes being repiped often have layouts that weren't designed with easy pipe-run access in mind, tight crawlspaces, finished basements, multiple floors. PEX's flexibility and ability to run in longer continuous sections without as many access points genuinely helps in these situations, which is part of why it's become the more common recommendation for whole-home repipes in this kind of housing stock.

That said, copper remains a completely legitimate choice, and some homeowners have specific reasons to prefer it, whether that's personal experience, resale considerations in certain markets, or simply a preference for a material with a longer history. We walk through both options honestly for your specific home's layout rather than defaulting to whichever is faster or more profitable for us to install.

A Note On Cost Over The Life Of The System

PEX's lower material and installation cost is real, but it's worth weighing against the full expected lifespan of either system rather than just the upfront number. Both materials, properly installed, are expected to serve a home for multiple decades, which means the practical cost difference over the system's full life is smaller than the upfront quote alone might suggest.

Where the upfront savings matter most is for homeowners working with a defined budget who need the full repipe completed now rather than phased over time. For those without that constraint, the choice more often comes down to genuine preference than a hard financial calculation either way.

What Happens To The Old Galvanized Pipe During A Repipe

Regardless of which new material you choose, the old galvanized pipe is fully removed and properly disposed of as part of the repipe, not simply capped off and left in place. Leaving old pipe abandoned in walls or crawlspaces isn't standard practice and can complicate future work in that space, so a proper repipe includes full removal of what's being replaced.

This is also the point in the process where we sometimes find things worth flagging beyond the pipe itself, an aging shutoff valve, a section of framing affected by a past slow leak. Those are worth addressing while the area is already open, rather than closing up and discovering the issue on a separate call later.

What Local Code Actually Allows

Both PEX and copper are recognized, approved materials under the Uniform Plumbing Code that Washington has adopted, so the decision between them isn't a code-compliance question, both are fully legitimate options for a residential repipe. Where code does matter is in the specific installation details: proper support spacing, correct fitting types, and appropriate transitions where the new material connects to any existing fixtures or pipe that isn't being replaced.

This is worth mentioning because occasionally a homeowner asks whether one material is more up to code than the other, and the honest answer is that neither has an inherent code advantage. The decision genuinely comes down to the practical factors covered above: installation approach for your home's specific layout, cost, and personal preference, not a compliance requirement pushing you toward one over the other.

Whichever material you choose, proper installation matters more to long-term performance than the material choice itself. A poorly installed repipe in the objectively better material for your situation will still underperform a properly installed repipe in the other, which is part of why the installer's skill matters just as much as the material selected. Ask about a contractor's specific experience with whichever material you're leaning toward, not just their general licensing credentials on file. Genuinely hands-on, repeated experience with a specific material meaningfully affects the overall quality and long-term durability of the finished installation itself, over its full working life.

We're happy to share examples of past repipe projects in either material if that would help you feel more confident in whichever direction you're leaning.
