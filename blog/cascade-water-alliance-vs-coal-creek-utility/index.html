Cascade Water Alliance vs. Coal Creek Utility District: Why Your Water Bill Looks Different

Most of the Eastside shares a water supplier. One nearby city doesn't. Here's the real difference.

By the Bellevue Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

Bellevue, Kirkland, Redmond, and Issaquah are all founding members of Cascade Water Alliance, a regional water supplier formed in 1999 that collectively serves more than 350,000 residents across the Eastside and beyond. If you've lived in more than one of these cities, your water bill has probably looked structurally similar each time, even if the exact rate differs.

Newcastle is the exception. It's served by Coal Creek Utility District instead, a separate special-purpose utility established in 1959 that predates Cascade Water Alliance entirely. If you've moved between Newcastle and one of its Cascade-served neighbors, the billing structure, contact information, and even the source water can look genuinely different, not just a different logo on the same system.

Where The Water Actually Comes From

Both systems ultimately draw from similar Cascade mountain sources managed by Seattle Public Utilities: the Cedar River and the South Fork Tolt River watersheds. Redmond is a partial exception, supplying roughly 40 percent of its own water from a local aquifer alongside the Seattle-sourced supply most of its neighbors rely on entirely. Coal Creek Utility District's water is drawn from the same Cedar River and Tolt River systems as Cascade's members, just delivered through a separate distribution system rather than through Cascade directly.

Why This Matters Beyond Just Billing

For most plumbing purposes, water hardness and treatment are similar enough across all these systems that it doesn't change how we diagnose or fix a problem. Where it does matter is if you're trying to report an issue, check for a water quality notice, or track down a billing question. Calling Bellevue's utility department about a Newcastle account, or the reverse, sends you to the wrong place entirely.

It's a small, practical thing to know if you own property in more than one of these cities, or if you're moving between them and setting up new utility service. The plumbing itself doesn't care which logo is on your water bill; getting the right utility on the phone when you actually need them does.

What Happens If You Move Between These Systems

If you're relocating from a Cascade-served city like Bellevue or Kirkland into Newcastle, or the reverse, setting up new water service means contacting a genuinely different organization, not just updating an account with the same provider under a different city name. Billing cycles, rate structures, and even the customer service contact information are separate between the two systems.

This is a minor administrative detail in the scheme of a move. But it's exactly the kind of thing that causes a brief service gap or billing confusion if you assume the transition works like moving between two Cascade member cities, where the underlying provider relationship doesn't actually change.

Why Regional Water Alliances Like This Exist At All

Cascade Water Alliance was formed specifically because no single Eastside city, on its own, had the scale to efficiently secure and manage a long-term water supply the way a larger regional partnership can. Pooling resources across Bellevue, Kirkland, Redmond, Issaquah, Tukwila, and the Sammamish Plateau lets the member cities negotiate supply agreements and fund major infrastructure more cheaply than any single city could alone. That's part of why water rates across those Cascade member cities tend to move together rather than diverging sharply.

Coal Creek Utility District takes a different structural approach, remaining a smaller, independent district rather than joining that regional alliance. In practice, that means Newcastle homeowners deal with a different rate structure and a different customer service line than their Cascade-served neighbors. Both still draw from the same underlying Cedar River and Tolt River source water, so the water itself isn't meaningfully different day to day.

What This Means If You're Comparing Properties To Buy

If you're house-hunting across Bellevue, Kirkland, and Newcastle, water utility provider isn't typically a deciding factor the way school district or commute time might be. But it's worth knowing which system a specific property falls under before you're dealing with a first water bill. A real estate agent working primarily in Cascade-member cities may not automatically flag the Newcastle difference unless you ask directly.

Property listings don't always specify water provider explicitly. If you're seriously considering a Newcastle property, Coal Creek Utility District's own website publishes current rates, service area maps, and water quality reports. That's a genuinely reasonable place to find real, current, accurate, and up-to-date specifics well beyond the general comparison already covered in this particular post above.

The same principle applies in reverse for someone moving out of Newcastle into a Cascade-served city: expect a genuinely new utility relationship to set up, not a simple account transfer, since the two systems don't share account infrastructure or billing history between them.

No. Coal Creek Utility District draws from the same Cedar River and Tolt River source systems as Cascade Water Alliance's members and publishes its own water quality reporting meeting the same federal and state standards.

In Newcastle, that's Coal Creek Utility District directly. In Bellevue and most other Eastside cities in this area, it's Cascade Water Alliance's member city utility department. They're genuinely separate organizations, not different names for the same one.

Coal Creek Utility District's roughly 5.7 square mile service area covers Newcastle and immediately surrounding unincorporated pockets, bounded by Bellevue to the north, Renton and Newcastle's own limits to the south, Lake Washington to the west, and Cougar Mountain Regional Wildland Park to the east.

Sammamish is served by Sammamish Plateau Water, which is itself a Cascade Water Alliance member, so it's a distinct local name but part of the same regional Cascade system Bellevue and Kirkland use, unlike Newcastle's fully separate Coal Creek Utility District.

Contact the utility that directly serves your address: your city's Cascade-affiliated utility department for Bellevue, Kirkland, Redmond, or Issaquah, Sammamish Plateau Water for Sammamish, or Coal Creek Utility District directly for Newcastle. Each handles its own billing and customer service independently, with its own phone line and account system.
