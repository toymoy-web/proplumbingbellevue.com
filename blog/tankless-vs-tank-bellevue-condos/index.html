Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters for Bellevue's Newer Condos

A real comparison for the dense, newer construction common downtown and around the Microsoft campus.

By the Bellevue Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

Downtown Bellevue and the Overlake area have added a lot of dense, newer housing over the past decade. A lot of that housing comes with real constraints a single-family home doesn't: limited mechanical space, shared walls, and sometimes stricter rules about what can go where. That changes the tank-versus-tankless conversation in a real way for condo owners.

Space Is Usually The Deciding Factor

A standard 40 or 50-gallon tank takes up real floor space that a condo mechanical closet often doesn't have to spare. A tankless unit mounts on a wall and takes a fraction of the footprint, which is why tankless systems are increasingly standard in newer Bellevue condo and townhome construction rather than an upgrade option.

Hot Water Demand In A Smaller Household

Many condo households are smaller than a typical single-family home, which changes the math on hot water demand too. A tank sized for a family of four running two showers back to back is often more capacity than a one or two-person condo household actually needs. A tankless unit scales to actual usage instead of a fixed tank size, without ever technically running out mid-shower.

What HOA Rules Sometimes Require

Some condo and townhome associations have specific requirements around water heater type, venting, or even brand, particularly in shared-wall construction where venting has to route through common areas. It's worth checking your HOA's rules before assuming you can install whichever system you prefer, since the mechanical constraints of dense construction sometimes come with matching association rules.

The Honest Trade-Off

Tankless costs more upfront and needs periodic descaling to stay efficient, particularly without a water softener. Tank systems cost less initially, are simpler to service, but take up space many newer condos don't have and run out of hot water during heavy simultaneous use. For most newer Bellevue condo construction, the space constraint alone tends to settle the decision toward tankless, but it's worth confirming based on your unit's actual mechanical closet rather than assuming.

What A Failed Tankless Unit Actually Means For A Condo Owner

When a tankless unit fails in a condo, the troubleshooting path is a bit different than in a single-family home, mostly because of shared venting and, sometimes, shared gas supply infrastructure that a single-family homeowner doesn't have to think about. Before assuming a unit itself has failed, it's worth confirming the issue isn't actually a building-level gas pressure or venting problem affecting multiple units.

This is exactly the kind of diagnostic step that benefits from a plumber who's already familiar with a specific building's mechanical systems. It's part of why we try to build that familiarity across the condo and townhome buildings we service repeatedly.

What To Ask Before Buying Into A Condo With An Unknown System

If you're touring a condo and the listing doesn't specify tank versus tankless, it's a reasonable question to ask directly, since the answer affects both your day-to-day hot water experience and your eventual replacement costs and timeline. A tank system nearing the end of its 10 to 12 year service life is a near-term expense worth factoring into your purchase decision; a newer tankless system generally has a longer runway before replacement.

It's also worth asking whether the unit is the building's original installation or has already been replaced once, since that tells you where in its expected lifespan the current system actually sits, information that isn't always obvious just from looking at the unit itself.

What A Condo Association's Master Insurance Policy Sometimes Requires

Some condo associations' master insurance policies include specific requirements around water heater installations, sometimes requiring a leak detection device or automatic shutoff valve alongside the unit. A water heater failure can cause much more damage in a shared-wall, multi-unit building than in a standalone single-family home.

It's worth checking your specific association's requirements before a new installation. A technically code-compliant installation that doesn't also meet your HOA's insurance-driven requirements can create a coverage gap you'd only discover after an actual failure, the worst possible time to find out.

We ask about HOA requirements as a standard question on any condo installation now. This gap has come up before, and it's far easier to address upfront than after a unit is already installed and a claim reveals the mismatch. A quick email or call to your HOA's management company before scheduling is usually all it takes to confirm there's no conflict, and we're glad to help coordinate that outreach. This kind of upfront coordination has saved more than one condo owner a genuinely unpleasant surprise discovered weeks later. That surprise typically comes after an otherwise straightforward installation was already fully complete and the replacement unit had already been back in regular daily household use for some time without a single reported issue of any kind. A short conversation upfront is a genuinely small, worthwhile price to pay for that kind of added peace of mind down the road. That's true for everyone genuinely involved in the entire condo installation and full building coordination process, from the very start all the way through to the final finish line.
