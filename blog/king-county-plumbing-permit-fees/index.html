What King County's Plumbing Permit Fees Actually Cost

Real numbers, not a vague 'it depends,' for what a permit adds to your next plumbing project.

By the Bellevue Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

Permit fees are one of the more opaque parts of a plumbing project for most homeowners, mostly because the actual cost depends on the number of fixtures involved and which jurisdiction is issuing the permit. Here's a straightforward breakdown of what's real and current, using Public Health Seattle & King County's published fee structure.

The Base Structure

King County's plumbing and backflow permit fee is built around a base application fee that covers administrative processing, plus an additional charge per fixture included in the project. As of the most recent published schedule, that's a base fee plus roughly $27 per fixture. A single water heater replacement, for example, is typically charged as one fixture on top of the base fee.

Requesting a change to an already-issued permit, like adding an additional fixture partway through a project, extending the permit, or changing contractors, involves its own process and sometimes its own fee, separate from the original permit charge.

What Counts As A Fixture

  • A water heater replacement or new installation.
  • A new or relocated sink, toilet, or tub.
  • A new floor drain.
  • A new or modified gas line connection.

The Owner-Builder Exemption

If you own and occupy your primary residence, King County generally allows you to pull your own plumbing permit and perform the work yourself under an owner-builder exemption, rather than requiring a licensed contractor to be the one applying. That exemption comes with real limits, most notably that the property can't be intended for immediate sale or rental, so it's worth confirming your specific situation qualifies before assuming it applies.

Why This Is Worth Budgeting For, Not Skipping

Unpermitted plumbing work, water heater replacements, sewer line work, and repipe projects especially, is one of the more common issues found during a real estate inspection, and it routinely delays closings or requires retroactive permitting and inspection before a sale can proceed. The permit fee is a small fraction of most plumbing project costs, and skipping it to save that amount tends to cost considerably more later, either at resale or if something goes wrong with unpermitted work.

When we handle a permitted job for you, the permit fee is built into the quote upfront, not added as a surprise later, and we manage the application and inspection scheduling as part of the work.

How Permit Fees Fit Into An Overall Project Quote

For most plumbing projects, the permit fee itself is a small fraction of the total cost, small enough that it rarely changes a homeowner's decision about whether to proceed. Where it matters more is in projects with many fixtures, a larger remodel or a multi-fixture repipe, where the per-fixture charges add up across the scope of the job.

When we quote a permitted project, the fee is included as a specific line item, not folded invisibly into a larger number, so you can see exactly what's going to the county versus what's covering labor and materials. That transparency is worth asking for from any contractor if a quote doesn't already break it out clearly.

What To Do If You Think A Fee Was Calculated Incorrectly

If a permit fee on your application looks higher than expected, the most common explanation is simply more fixtures being counted than you anticipated, sometimes because a project's scope expanded slightly from the original plan. It's worth reviewing the itemized fixture list against what's actually being installed before assuming an error, since a genuine miscount is less common than a scope difference.

If you do believe there's a genuine calculation error, King County's permit portal allows you to request a review or correction directly, and a licensed contractor handling your permit application will typically manage that conversation on your behalf as part of the service.

How Permit Timelines Typically Work

For a straightforward, over-the-counter permit like a standard water heater replacement, approval is often same-day or close to it. More involved projects, a full repipe, new construction rough-in, or anything requiring plan review rather than a standard checklist, take longer, sometimes several weeks depending on the county's current review volume and the project's complexity.

Scheduling a project around a realistic permit timeline, rather than assuming everything moves at over-the-counter speed, avoids the frustration of a project stalling on paperwork rather than actual plumbing work. We factor expected permit timelines into project scheduling from the start, so a homeowner isn't caught off guard by a review period they didn't anticipate.

For a time-sensitive project, like a real estate closing deadline, it's worth flagging that urgency at the very start of the permit process rather than discovering partway through the review that a standard timeline won't meet your actual closing deadline. We've helped clients navigate exactly this kind of time-sensitive permit situation before. We can't guarantee a specific turnaround from the county's own review process, but flagging the urgency early in the process genuinely does help us move things along as fast as reasonably possible on our own scheduling end.

Real estate closings involving plumbing permit contingencies are common enough on the Eastside that most title companies and agents are already quite familiar with this exact timeline conversation well ahead of the actual closing date.
