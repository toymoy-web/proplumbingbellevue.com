Grease Interceptor Rules for Bellevue Restaurants

A straightforward look at what Bellevue's sewer code and the city's grease guidance ask of commercial kitchens.

By the Bellevue Pro Plumbing team · September 2026 · Updated October 2026

If you're running a commercial kitchen in Bellevue, a working grease interceptor isn't optional. Bellevue's sewer code requires restaurants and food processing businesses to install pretreatment such as grease interceptors, and the city's own guidance says those interceptors should be cleaned regularly.

Why This Is A Code Requirement, Not Just Good Practice

Grease that goes down a commercial kitchen drain without an interceptor eventually reaches the public sewer system, where it can cause blockages far beyond your own building. Those blockages can back sewage into other properties and public infrastructure. That's the underlying reason this is regulated rather than left to individual business discretion.

Grease Interceptors In Bellevue

In Bellevue the city's sewer utility sets the rules. Section 24.04.213 of the Bellevue City Code says restaurants and food processing businesses shall install pretreatment methods, such as grease interceptors, and that pretreatment systems are subject to review and approval by the utility. Another section directs utility customers to comply with applicable King County department requirements as well.

The city's fats, oils and grease guidance says all grease interceptors should be cleaned at least twice each year. It also says the Health Department makes periodic inspections and describes the fullness test above. That document is undated, so confirm the current cleaning frequency with the city's industrial waste coordinator before you build a schedule around it.

Sources: Bellevue City Code 24.04.213 and 24.04.240, City of Bellevue fats, oils and grease FAQ.

What Counts As Compliance

  • A properly sized grease interceptor for your kitchen's actual volume and type of food service.
  • Regular cleaning on a schedule that matches your kitchen's real grease output, not just an arbitrary calendar interval.
  • Records of service dates, volume removed and any issues noted during cleaning. No rule we found spells out record keeping, but a clean paper trail helps in any dispute.
  • An interceptor that's accessible for both regular service and inspector review.

How Often Cleaning Is Actually Required

Bellevue's grease guidance says interceptors should be cleaned at least twice each year. Many kitchens need service more often, and the city's inspection method is based on how full the interceptor is, not on the calendar. A high-volume kitchen can reach a failing level well before the next scheduled visit.

What Happens If You're Not Compliant

An undersized, poorly maintained or neglected interceptor eventually backs up, which is disruptive to your business regardless of the code question. Beyond the immediate problem, the city can issue a compliance order requiring an overfull interceptor to be cleaned immediately. Neither outcome is worth the modest, predictable cost of staying on a proper schedule.

If you're opening a new kitchen or taking over an existing space, it's worth having your interceptor's sizing reviewed against your actual expected volume before you assume the existing setup is adequate for how you'll actually be using the kitchen.

What A Typical Inspection Actually Checks

Bellevue's grease guidance describes an inspection that looks at how full the interceptor is. An interceptor that is 25 percent full or less rates as good, and one that is more than 50 percent full rates as poor. Poor results lead to a compliance order to clean it immediately.

Kitchens that stay consistently compliant treat this as a routine operating item, the same way they track any other recurring maintenance need. Keeping straightforward, current service records on file means you are never scrambling when someone asks.

What New Restaurant Owners Get Wrong Most Often

The most common mistake we see with new restaurant openings isn't ignoring the requirement entirely. It's underestimating the interceptor size needed for the kitchen's actual planned volume. This often happens because sizing was based on a generic recommendation rather than the specific menu and expected covers per day. An undersized system that seemed adequate on paper often shows real strain within the first few months of actual operation.

Getting sizing reviewed against your real, specific kitchen plan before installation, rather than after you're already dealing with an interceptor that can't keep pace, is the more cost-effective approach, even though it takes a bit more planning upfront.

How This Applies To A Shared Or Commissary Kitchen

Clear agreements about maintenance responsibility and cost sharing matter here in a way they don't for a single-operator kitchen.

If you're entering a shared kitchen arrangement, confirm in writing who is responsible for scheduling and paying for grease interceptor service. Confirm too that the existing interceptor is sized for the combined volume of all operators using the space. A short walkthrough with every operator present is a low cost step that avoids disputes after a backup disrupts several businesses at once.

We've seen shared kitchens run into real problems specifically because this wasn't clarified upfront, with each operator assuming another was handling service until a backup made the gap obvious. A short conversation before signing on to a shared space avoids that entirely.
