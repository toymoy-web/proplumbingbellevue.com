Do You Need a Permit to Replace a Water Heater in Bellevue?

King County changed its rule on this in 2026. Here's what actually applies to a Bellevue homeowner right now.

By the Bellevue Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

Until recently, a straightforward, same-location water heater swap in most of King County didn't require a permit. That changed. Permits are now required for replacement water heaters starting July 1, 2026, and in most cases it's an over-the-counter plumbing permit rather than a longer plan-review process. If you're a Bellevue homeowner planning a water heater replacement, this is worth knowing before you schedule anything.

What Actually Triggers The Permit Requirement

The short version: replacing a water heater in the same location, tank for tank or tank for tankless, now needs a permit under the updated King County rule. This applies whether you're doing the work yourself or hiring a licensed plumber. It's a change from the older, looser standard where a like-for-like swap sometimes flew under the radar.

Minor repairs still don't require a permit. Clearing a clog, fixing a leaky faucet, or swapping a fixture like a toilet or sink in the exact same location generally stays permit-free. The line gets crossed when you're adding new rough-in plumbing, relocating a fixture, or extending piping, and a water heater replacement now falls on the permit side of that line regardless of relocation.

What A Permit Actually Costs

King County's plumbing and backflow permit fee runs a base charge plus a per-fixture fee, adjusted periodically. Expect a base fee in the neighborhood of the current published schedule plus an additional charge for the water heater itself as a fixture. It's a modest add to the overall job, but it's a real cost that wasn't part of the conversation a couple of years ago.

Seismic Bracing Is Still Required, Permit Or Not

Separate from the new permit rule, Washington has long required seismic bracing on water heaters, installed with earthquake risk specifically in mind. The tank needs two straps: one on the upper third and one on the lower third. If your existing water heater doesn't have this, or has bracing that's come loose or corroded, that's worth fixing during any service visit, permit or not.

  • A permit is now required for a same-location water heater replacement in King County, effective July 1, 2026.
  • Minor repairs, clog clearing, and same-location fixture swaps generally stay permit-free.
  • Seismic bracing (two straps, upper and lower third of the tank) is a separate, ongoing Washington code requirement.
  • A gas water heater still needs its own gas piping permit if the gas line is being modified.

Can A Homeowner Pull Their Own Permit?

Yes, for a primary residence, King County generally allows a homeowner to pull their own plumbing permit under an owner-builder exemption, with the caveat that the property can't be intended for immediate sale or rental under that exemption. If you hire a licensed plumber instead, we handle the permit as part of the job rather than leaving it to you to navigate separately.

For most Bellevue homeowners, the practical upshot is small: a water heater replacement now includes a permit step it didn't always include before, and the inspection that follows confirms the installation meets current code, including that seismic bracing requirement. It's a minor addition to the process, not a reason to delay a replacement you actually need.

How This Compares To Other Recent Permit Changes

This isn't the first time King County has tightened a plumbing permit requirement that used to be looser in practice. Sewer line repairs and full repipe projects have required permits for years. The water heater change essentially closes a gap where a genuinely significant mechanical installation, one involving pressurized water, gas or electricity, and venting, had been treated more casually than its actual risk profile warranted.

For homeowners who've replaced a water heater before under the old, looser standard, the most common point of confusion is simply not realizing the rule changed. If your last replacement was more than a year or two ago, don't assume the process works the same way this time; it's worth confirming current requirements rather than going by what worked last time.

What The Inspection Itself Looks For

Once the permit is pulled and the installation is complete, an inspector checks a specific list of items. That includes correct venting for the fuel type, proper gas line sizing, seismic bracing in the upper and lower third of the tank, a correctly routed relief valve, and an expansion tank where required. None of this is arbitrary; each item corresponds to a real failure mode the code is designed to prevent.

Most straightforward, correctly done installations pass on the first inspection without issue. The installations that fail tend to fail on one of a small handful of recurring items, most commonly missing or improperly anchored seismic bracing, or a missing expansion tank on a closed system. Both are quick to get right the first time if you know to check for them.
