Grease Trap Rules for Bellevue Restaurants: What King County Actually Requires

A straightforward look at the real health code requirement, not a vague compliance warning.

By the Bellevue Pro Plumbing team · September 2026

If you're running a commercial kitchen in Bellevue or anywhere else in King County, a functioning grease interceptor isn't optional, and it isn't just a plumbing best practice. It's a health code requirement, and inspectors do check maintenance records as part of routine inspections, not just when something has already gone wrong.

Why This Is A Code Requirement, Not Just Good Practice

Grease that goes down a commercial kitchen drain without an interceptor eventually reaches the public sewer system, where it can cause blockages far beyond just your own building, contributing to sewer backups that affect other properties and public infrastructure. That's the underlying reason this is regulated rather than left to individual business discretion.

What Counts As Compliance

  • A properly sized grease interceptor for your kitchen's actual volume and type of food service.
  • Regular cleaning on a schedule that matches your kitchen's real grease output, not just an arbitrary calendar interval.
  • Maintained records of service dates, volume removed, and any issues noted during cleaning.
  • An interceptor that's accessible for both regular service and inspector review.

How Often Cleaning Is Actually Required

There's no single universal number, since it genuinely depends on your kitchen's volume and the interceptor's size. Most commercial kitchens land somewhere between monthly and quarterly service, but a high-volume operation might need more frequent attention while a lower-volume one might reasonably stretch the interval. What matters for compliance is that the schedule matches your actual output, documented, not that you're hitting an arbitrary number.

What Happens If You're Not Compliant

An undersized, poorly maintained, or neglected interceptor eventually backs up, which is disruptive to your business regardless of the code question. Beyond the immediate operational problem, a health inspection that finds inadequate documentation or an obviously neglected system can result in citations or, in more serious cases, affect your operating status. Neither outcome is worth the modest, predictable cost of staying on a proper schedule.

If you're opening a new kitchen or taking over an existing space, it's worth having your interceptor's sizing reviewed against your actual expected volume before you assume the existing setup is adequate for how you'll actually be using the kitchen.

What A Typical Inspection Actually Checks

A health inspector reviewing grease trap compliance is generally looking at three things. Is the interceptor appropriately sized for the kitchen's actual operation? Is it being serviced on a documented, realistic schedule? And are current maintenance records available on request rather than reconstructed after the fact?

Kitchens that stay consistently compliant tend to treat this as a routine operational item, the same way they'd track any other recurring maintenance need, rather than scrambling to pull records together only when an inspection is announced. Keeping straightforward, current service records on file is most of what's actually required here.

What New Restaurant Owners Get Wrong Most Often

The most common mistake we see with new restaurant openings isn't ignoring the requirement entirely; it's underestimating the interceptor size needed for the kitchen's actual planned volume. This often happens because sizing was based on a generic recommendation rather than the specific menu and expected covers per day. An undersized system that seemed adequate on paper often shows real strain within the first few months of actual operation.

Getting sizing reviewed against your real, specific kitchen plan before installation, rather than after you're already dealing with an interceptor that can't keep pace, is the more cost-effective approach, even though it takes a bit more planning upfront.

How This Applies To A Shared Or Commissary Kitchen

Shared commercial kitchen spaces, where multiple food businesses operate out of the same physical facility, add a layer of complexity to grease trap compliance, since the interceptor is typically shared infrastructure serving multiple operators rather than one business's sole responsibility. Clear agreements about maintenance responsibility and cost-sharing matter here in a way they don't for a single-operator kitchen.

If you're entering a shared kitchen arrangement, it's worth confirming in writing who's responsible for scheduling and paying for grease trap service. Confirm too that the existing interceptor is actually sized for the combined volume of all operators using the space, not just what it was originally sized for under a prior single tenant. A quick walkthrough with all current operators present, comparing actual usage against the interceptor's rated capacity, is a reasonable, low-cost step before any dispute comes up. We've mediated exactly this kind of shared-responsibility conversation before. It's almost always considerably easier to resolve proactively and clearly upfront than after a real backup has already disrupted multiple different businesses sharing that same commercial kitchen space, equipment, and drain infrastructure. That's especially true during an already busy, high-pressure commercial kitchen service period, for everyone genuinely involved in the shared, ongoing, day-to-day daily kitchen operations of that busy, active commercial space.

We've seen shared kitchens run into real problems specifically because this wasn't clarified upfront, with each operator assuming another was handling service until a backup made the gap obvious. A short conversation before signing on to a shared space avoids that entirely.
